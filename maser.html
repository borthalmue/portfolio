What is a Maser (how does it connect to Microwaves)?

A maser (short for Microwave Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation) is a device that produces and amplifies electromagnetic waves, typically in the microwave or radio frequency range, through the process of stimulated emission. It operates on principles similar to a laser, but instead of emitting light, it emits coherent microwaves or radio waves — a laser is essentially a version of a maser operating at optical frequencies instead of microwave frequencies.1

Microwaves interact with matter through a transfer in energy, often resulting in heat. They are especially effective at exciting polar molecules in water, fats, and sugars, which carry positive and negative charges.2 When microwaves pass through polar materials, the molecules rapidly rotate and vibrate, creating friction and heat at the molecular level.3 That heat is then naturally distributed throughout the material — the same mechanism that makes microwaves efficient for heating or cooking food.

Masers are a relatively new technology — the first room-temperature maser was only produced in 2012. That opened up new opportunities for masers in a variety of spaces, including the medical field.4

Precautions with Medical Devices Not Compatible with Masers

Microwave maser technology creates high-intensity microwave radiation that, when precisely focused, can create localized heating of target tissue — leading to controlled destruction or coagulation of abnormal cells.5 That precision comes with risk: there is a real possibility of collateral damage to surrounding healthy tissue,6 and the intense electromagnetic fields a maser produces can interfere with the operation of various sensitive medical technologies. High-frequency radiation can interfere with imaging systems like MRIs and CT scanners, potentially distorting images or producing inaccurate results.7 Masers fortunately do not affect X-rays, which allows a system to use X-ray imaging to locate the coordinates of the area that needs to be cauterized or operated on.8

Masers can also pose a significant risk to implanted medical devices such as pacemakers, defibrillators, and neurostimulators, which rely on precise electrical signals. Exposure to microwave radiation can cause malfunctions, alter device programming, or induce erratic behavior.9 Devices that use RF communication may also experience signal interference or loss of communication. Because of these risks, strict precautions — shielding, calibration, and keeping adequate distance from critical devices — are required in any clinical setting using maser technology.

Implanted Medical Devices

  • Pacemakers & Defibrillators: microwaves can break electrical circuitry, potentially causing dangerous misfiring.
  • Cochlear Implants: microwaves can create signal disruption or damage.10
  • Neurostimulators: high-energy radiation could interfere with the device's electrical output or programming.11

Wearable or Monitoring Devices

  • Heart Rate / Glucose Monitors: microwaves could disrupt wireless communication or internal electronics.
  • Insulin Pumps: exposure could lead to malfunctions in delivery mechanisms or electronics.12

Metallic / Electronic Prosthetics

  • Myoelectric Prosthetics: sensitive electronic components could overheat or short-circuit.13
  • Metal Implants (rods, screws, braces): metal reflects or absorbs microwaves, causing localized heating or potential burns.14

Life Support & Diagnostic Devices

  • Ventilators / ECMO: disrupted electronic controls could lead to failure.
  • Dialysis Machines: potential overheating or electronic damage.15
  • Portable Ultrasound: interference with signal processing or probe damage.
  • EKG Machines: disruption of electrical signals may lead to inaccurate readings or device failure.16

Microwave Effects on Different Internal Tissues

Tissue TypeKey Properties & Reaction to Masers
MuscleHigh water content — absorbs energy efficiently.
FatLower water content — less absorption, better transparency.
BoneDense and reflective — blocks microwaves significantly.
BloodAbsorbs energy well due to high water content.
Brain TissueHigh water content and protected by the skull — highly absorbent, but bone increases attenuation. Excessive heating risks neurological disruption or localized cell death.

Energy Attenuation by Tissue Type

Each tissue in the body reduces maser energy by a different amount, depending on water content, density, and blood flow. This must be accounted for when focusing on internal targets and calculating required energy levels.

Tissue LayerEnergy Attenuation Rate (per cm)
Skin10–20%
Fat5–10%
Muscle15–25%
Bone30–50%
Brain Tissue20–40%

Cumulative attenuation is found by summing the reduction across layers:17

E = (1 − a)ⁿ

Where E is the fraction of energy remaining after passing through the tissue, a is the attenuation rate as a decimal, and n is the number of centimeters traveled.

For example, at 3 cm into muscle tissue (attenuation ≈ 20%/cm), roughly 51% of the beam's original energy remains — illustrating why deeper or bone-shielded targets require substantially higher initial output.

Energy Requirements

To cauterize tissue, the focal point must reach approximately 80°C. A single maser beam loses roughly 33% of its energy per centimeter when passing through bone — meaning reaching a focal point 18 inches (45.72 cm) into the body may require a total of 1,372 kilowatts of pulsed power from all four masers combined.18

FDA and IEC 60601-1-2 Standards for Radiation and Energy Levels

Compliance with IEC 60601-1-2 is critical for electromagnetic safety — it specifies allowable limits for emissions and immunity to electromagnetic disturbance so a device doesn't interfere with other medical equipment.19,20 Thermal safety is governed by Specific Absorption Rate (SAR): for localized applications like internal cauterization, SAR should not exceed 1.6 W/kg averaged over 1 gram of tissue (U.S.), or 4 W/kg internationally — limits meant to keep tissue below the ~43°C threshold for irreversible thermal harm during prolonged exposure.

Energy levels for internal cauterization devices are typically controlled to 10–100 watts at frequencies like 915 MHz or 2.45 GHz, as used in microwave ablation systems today. ICNIRP guidelines cap pulsed public exposure around 10 mW/cm², with higher allowances for occupational settings.21,24 The maser system also follows ISO 14971 for risk management — identifying, assessing, and mitigating electrical, thermal, and mechanical failure modes, targeting a failure rate under 0.1% per year to meet medical-device reliability expectations.23

Energy Transfer Simulation & Thermal Effect Calculations

To estimate the temperature rise in tissue from absorbed energy:

Q = m · c · ΔT

Where Q is heat energy absorbed (J), m is tissue mass (g), c is specific heat capacity (J/g°C), and ΔT is the temperature change (°C).

TissueSpecific Heat (J/g°C)Density (g/cm³)Thermal Conductivity (W/m°C)
Muscle3.81.60.51
Fat2.10.920.19
Bone1.41.851.30
Blood3.81.060.52
Brain Tissue3.91.040.49

As a worked example: 40 W absorbed in 5 g of muscle tissue (c = 3.8 J/g°C) over 2 seconds delivers Q = 80 J, for a temperature rise of ΔT ≈ 10.1°C — a reminder of how quickly focused pulses can push tissue toward the cauterization threshold if left uncontrolled.

Regulatory Compliance for Pulse Duration and Frequency

IEC standards for medical equipment may stipulate a maximum pulse duration of 1 millisecond and a maximum pulse energy of 10 millijoules, with a duty cycle that prevents prolonged high-intensity pulses.28

E(pulse) = P(avg) · τ
P(total) = E(pulse) · f(rep)

Where τ is pulse duration (s) and f(rep) is the pulse repetition frequency (pulses/s). Compliance requires confirming E(pulse) ≤ E(max), τ ≤ τ(max), and P(total) ≤ P(max).

Maser Interference Intensity & Focal Point Dynamics

For each maser beam, the electric field at a point in space and time is:

E₁(total) = E₀cos(wt + ø₁)
E₂(total) = E₀cos(wt + ø₂)

Where E₀ is the field amplitude (proportional to maser energy), w = 2πf is angular frequency, and ø₁/ø₂ are the starting phase angles. The phase difference (ø₁ − ø₂) determines whether the waves interfere constructively or destructively at the focal point.29,30

P(abs) ∝ [2E·cos(2Δø)·cos(ωt + 2ø₁ + ø₂)]²
ΔT = P(abs)·t / (m·C)

At the focal point, constructive interference (phase difference = 0 or an integer multiple of 2π) maximizes amplitude — this is the region the maser system targets to concentrate heating precisely at the treatment site while minimizing effects elsewhere.31

How the Medical Device Works

A key feature distinguishing masers from traditional microwave technologies is their ability to emit highly concentrated pulses of energy, enabling extreme precision. Pulses are delivered at high energy levels — up to 300 kilowatts — allowing rapid targeting and heating of tumors or problem tissue to the ~80°C cauterization threshold, released in brief millisecond bursts so surrounding tissue can dissipate excess heat between pulses.32–34

Different tissues absorb energy differently, so the system dynamically adjusts pulse strength and timing — in highly vascularized tissue, this enables quick coagulation of blood vessels while preventing heat buildup in surrounding healthy tissue.35,36

Operational Workflow

The target tissue is first identified using imaging (X-ray or ultrasound) to pinpoint the exact focal coordinates. The maser system's mounts are then positioned to focus their beams on that point. Throughout the procedure, real-time imaging and temperature sensors monitor tissue response — if the target reaches the desired cauterization level, energy output is reduced or halted; if not, the system increases energy or adjusts pulse timing to compensate. For deeply embedded or bone-shielded tissue, the system can add pulses or adjust frequency to penetrate more effectively.38,39

Conclusion

While converging multiple maser beams to a focal point is technically possible with current technology, several significant challenges remain. Achieving precise wave synchronization and power control to concentrate heat in a controlled, targeted manner would require advanced phase-locking techniques and real-time feedback to fine-tune the system during operation. Careful thermal management is essential to deliver focused energy safely without damaging surrounding tissue, and the precision required for mounting, aligning, and dynamically adjusting the beams demands significant engineering expertise. Regulatory hurdles for a novel technology like this would also require substantial additional research and testing before clinical adoption. The technology is within reach — but a safe, practical medical system is an ambitious, multi-year engineering project, requiring far more R&D before even a working prototype is realistic.
